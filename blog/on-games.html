On games.

May 18, 2025

The following is based on a true story — or at least on what I perceive as truth.

I’m back outside in the glorious spring sunshine, on the bricks of Pike Street. I make my way northwest. It’s a Wednesday and the shops have seen their height of attention and commerce and selfies and conversations with passersby. Through Post Alley, I find a beautiful waitress smoking in the sliver of sunlight that two buildings make way for. She smiles at the drag, and then returns to it, renewed. Underfoot are the ashes of the day’s already forgotten frustrations.

In a place beyond, I see Jim and friends, enjoying a sniff of air and some conversation. Jim welcomes me warmly with a hug. “Ahhh,” he says, “Andres, maker of delicious desserts.”

He introduces me to the core squad. I remember Andy (Alimbuyuguen), who will later go on to interview Ben (Roth). The others are kind, shake my hand, and jokingly tell me there will be a quiz in 15 minutes where I’ll have to recall all of their names. From the other end of the alley, a wild Jack appears. Jack looks like Bob Ross if Bob Ross wears cargo pants and writes code. Jim says, “This is Jack. Jack Dorsey, creator of Twitter.”

Some small talk is exchanged along with a book, and a nod to the art displayed in the studio front window. It’s Anna’s Bead Bodega, which I’ll go on to describe in detail later. One of the entourage says, “It’s nice that the display is moveable.” I ask, “How often do you switch out the artists?” With conviction, Jim interjects, “I’ll switch it when someone has more balls than Anna!”

Ben arrives and speaks of Borges and the various versions of self that a man may have. There is some talk of games and film and young media talent hidden away in the bowels of Seattle. Oh, and the imminent demise of Disney. Jim says, of the event, “I hope this is a platform to ask audacious questions, honest questions about how we can build great games.”

Eventually, a critical mass of people moves indoors and Ben suggests we do the same. I find plenty of people inside and am greeted by a gentleman named Blake, an investor from J.P. Morgan. He asks me to grab a name tag and efficiently moves on to greet the folks coming in behind me. The name tags rest upon a placard that reads:

Noble art of web creation, do hereby proclaim this vow to guide our minds. The past, though a tutor, is not a thing to lament, but a wellspring of wisdom from which we draw without regret. A measure of obsession doth sharpen the will, for the long path, though beset with trials, leadeth ever toward glories. Kindness and gratitude must light our way in all dealings, regardless of circumstance. Stand firm beside thy fellow for shared triumph is the sweetest of all. Let us strive that design be not a mere ornament to the world, but a worthy service to humanity itself. Internet artifacts built with care do grant webmasters the power to shape their own fates, and to serve those thou dost cherish is an honor beyond measure. Intensity, as fire contained, burneth most brightly when turned toward aims; thus, let us spurn the call of mediocrity and cast aside the seductions of decay. Mistakes are not curses, but treasures wrapped in adversity, offered to those wise enough to seize their lessons. Expect naught, but receive all with gratitude, and find a place of trust wherein thou mayest dwell for a time. From thy master learn what thou canst, and from all others likewise, for wisdom hath many guises. To follow with discipline is good; to follow oneself with discipline is better. Work with fervor and take joy where it may be found, for the making is as sweet as the outcome. Be mindful that there are ever more ways than two, and seek not merely to follow the trade but to alter its course, bringing value wherever it is given thee to do so. Whether rewarded or not, let us ply our craft for the love of it, for therein lies mastery. Assume nothing, and let no person escape thy regard. A fine process doth honor the task, but let the result be no less fine. Keep faith with those who make promises, and shouldst thou be called to push others, push thyself the harder. Thy health is thy treasure, and building a company shall be no graceful dance, but a contest of strife. Take always the first step, for even in failure doth wisdom grow. Seek ever to broaden thy knowledge of art, software, music, letters, and all things ancient and new, for these are the treasures of the mind. Thy fate lieth ever in thine own hands, and the soul readeth not only the landscape, but the heart that beats beneath it. Let thy voice be thine own, unborrowed and unbowed, and make it heard without fear. Rejoice in the victories of thy fellow webmasters, for in bearing witness to their triumphs is joy in its truest form. Grow accustomed to the weight of pressure, for in such a forge is the finest steel tempered. Thus do we press onward —undaunted and unyielding— in pursuit of a better physical and digital world.

I look around and realize I don’t know anyone. It’s a scary feeling. I find myself feeling suddenly very awkward and out of place. I reach for my phone, but remember that there’s nothing there for me that’s more interesting than the community which unfurls before mine very eyes. And most intriguing to me is that they all have one thing in mind: games.

I stash my backpack inside a large wooden frame structure of some sort. I know not what the structure is. But I discern from the tools — drill, bits, bubble levels, screws, saws — that it’s none other than, a work in progress. I look over to the Bead Bodega, and back to this structure. My mind wonders what it is that will be expressed by this timber, that which smells still fresh of forest and fern and the outside, brought in by one or many who inspired air enough to take action on notion.

The space exudes a sense of genesis and openness of intentionality, from the way that the chairs are thoughtfully arranged, to the way the lighting casts warm shadows on the walls and ceiling. In the front of the room there is a sofa that looks as if it were made of moss. Regardless of it’s composition, it’s surely a seat that may swallow one whole. A place for lost wonderers to stop and rest a while, and consider what it is that they make of all this, this life, happening all at once, all around us. Atop a side table sits a book on whose spine is stamped a name in bold and all caps: DIETER RAMS. And of course, next to the book is a hamburger. It’s a toy and looks how I would imagine a burger to appear in a video game.

Because I’m a nerd and socially anxious, I sit in the front row, before anyone else, and simply wait for the discussion to begin. To pass the time, I breathe deeply and retreat inward. I open my notebook and begin writing what I deem to be my greatest, present curiosities:

Why would you build games? What’s the point?

What’s your relationship with “play?” How does that relationship inform the way you think about building games?

Who do you design for? Do you have a clear audience or player-persona in mind when creating games?

To what degree is the player an emergent property of the game design process?

How will AI change the fabric of gaming? On the build-side? On the play-side?

What’s hype and what’s actual fire in gaming, 2025? What are the pre-eminent opportunities in game development right now?

What part about making games absolutely sucks ass? What part brings you the most joy?

What’s bad advice you hear people giving those who are trying to get into gaming or building games? What should they ignore?

What’s your dream? When you envision the future of gaming, what do you see in your mind’s eye?

Has competitive gaming and e-sports changed the way we have to think about game design? If so, how? Why?

How do we get more people building games? What’s the first step for noobs?

I pause and wonder what new curiosities will emerge as the night’s events unfold. Before long, someone grabs the seat next to me. Or perhaps the seat next to the seat next to me. I sense his presence and snap out of the reverie. I blurt out a very Midwestern, “Heya, how’s it goin’?” He’s drinking coffee contemplatively at 6pm and responds to me with ten times more energy than I expect. Something tells me it’s not the coffee. His name is Sai (Narayan) and he happens to be one of tonight’s panelists. We chit chat about what brings us to the space and I learn that Sai is an engineer and technical artists who works for Unity. However, I quickly glean that the Unity gig is in service of something far more interesting to him — Project Gilgamesh. He tells me it’s as if the parkour of Prince of Persia had a baby with Beat Saber. It’s basically the ultimate infinite runner where you move with the rhythm of some sick-nasty music to enter a flow state. This game has been his brain child for many years now and he’s built a team of technical experts to help him breathe life into this vision. Without a doubt this dude is very hard working and crazy humble. I discern this from the way he speaks praises about his teammates. His animation and narrative director, Madeline (aka Maddie) (Bledsoe), is on her way and will be joining Sai on the moss couch.

Before long, Jim makes a booming announcement for everyone to find a seat. Short stature, big heart, big voice. He keeps his opening remarks concise and overflowing with gratitude. He says to the room with pride, “Thanks for coming. My name’s Jim and I’m the studio janitor. I have many bosses here and they’re all younger than me.” He tells us the purpose of INTDEV: to build fine things together with friends and to always try our best.

First up in the moss is a guy named Jeff (Zhang). Jim explains how he and Jeff met serendipitously at a meetup for mechanical keyboard nerds, which seems to me to be completely on brand. Turns out Jeff’s been dabbling in games for a long long time. He tells a story from his youth, when he and a group of friends who lived in various countries around the world effectively monopolized the global leaderboard of a computer game by meticulously trading shifts, tracked in a spreadsheet, whereby each of them took turns staying awake to beat the big, bad boss or whatever. It’s clear that this experience sparked some desire deep within him. Since then, Jeff has gamed competitively, and led UX at Riot for a little ditty called League of Legends — a game that once made a college roommate of mine nocturnal. Thanks for that, Jeff.

Jeff now works as a game designer at a studio called Theorycraft Games. He describes the creation of a video game as a group art project where creative desire and vision are in constant flux and negotiation. “It’s not always easy,” he says, as he laughs about recent yelling matches with teammates he loves. But like any relationship, it’s all a matter of give and take, of compromise. Sometimes you advocate for your idea, only to see it crash and burn. Other times you step out of the way to let the homies take the wheel, and pursue something that inspires them. Trust is the key ingredient, and will come up in later discussion.

Jeff also describes gaming as an act of learning. Namely, the process of intuiting something through the game itself. While this intuition is highly sought after, it can sometimes be a blocker for game designers. He says, “As someone who’s been working on a project for so long, the fun is obvious to us, the team. But the real goal is to see if the fun is even remotely accessible to other people.”

Moreover, he mentioned that VCs don’t give a rats ass about “fun.” They care about return on investment. Jeff speaks of a lucky time, back in 2018–2019, when investment dollars flowed abundantly. Or as he puts it, “You could ask for a fuck ton of money to make some shit no one’s ever seen or heard of before.” But he explains that VCs often miss the mark and “confuse some level of academic or professional pedigree for relevant experience in game design.” Personally, Jeff would rather bet on younger, less-resourced teams. But one thing is true regardless: game developers can no longer solicit investments based on a wish, a vision, a dream. Blake concurs. “If you want to raise money, you have to prove your differentiation from everyone else.” And there are lots of “everyone else.” Check out Jeff’s work at playsupervive.com.

Next in the big couch is Paul (Frazee), developer at Bluesky, a decentralized, microblogging social media platform. Paul’s wearing sandals and I think to myself it’s still too cold for my little piggies. This guy might be built different. Although Bluesky is not a video game per se, Paul explains that social media has inherent game loops. For instance, social validation in the form of “likes serve as similar purpose as racking up points in a game.” Comments allow you to interact with other people, and “going viral” gives people the dopamine rush they need to keep playing the game.

Paul speaks a lot about the process of developing Bluesky into what it is today. He describes something he calls the “percent easy” framework. “If it doesn’t feel like the wind is at your back after doing the work, it might be time to move on.” In other words, “If it’s not 90% easy, it’s probably terrible.” This speaks to the futility of doing something that doesn’t make sense. Knowing when to quit is as valuable as knowing when to start. In fact, ends and beginnings are one and the same.

When it comes to starting, Paul emphasizes the importance of starting small. To figure out if something is worthwhile, you have to take action. Start with a prototype, a mini project, and see how it goes. Even more insightful is Paul’s belief that these experiments are best conducted with other people. He describes “co-founder dating” as working alongside new people with the intention of finding teammates you respect. Mutual respect, in Paul’s estimation, is the key ingredient in a great builder relationship.

After Paul, Jim is joined on the mossy throne by Sai (Narayan) and Maddie (Bledsoe), whom I mentioned earlier. Now they’re in front of the room, and Maddie describes the work she does as animation and narrative director of Mesh Collective, a 5-person game dev studio currently trying to take Project Gilgamesh to the next level. She joined Sai after leaving an earlier career in TV, film, and media (more on that later). The gift of serendipity makes another appearance, this time in her story. Sai and Maddie met randomly at a game dev meetup of some sort and from curiosity flowed the realization that they might work well together. After working on Project Gilgamesh alone for so long, Sai realized, “With enough time and effort, I could, in theory, solve any problem. But I learned, I’m actually not as smart as I think. I needed a team of the best of the best builders to help me.” He adds, looking to Maddie, “great teammates are curious, persistent, collaborative, and trustworthy. And they have excellent taste!” This makes me think a recent interview I watched with Rick Rubin where he stresses the importance of developing your sense of taste and ability to spot great taste in other artists. Sai exclaims, “Find people to work with whose taste you trust!”

At this point he cites a Paul Graham essay called How to Do Great Work. This piece speaks to the value of earnestly and consistently following your curiosity. Sai shares, “The thing I’ve had to learn the most is patience.”

Maddie goes on to speak to the power of narrative in games and how players really just want to feel like they’re part of the drama. I’ll go on to have a deeper discussion with Maddie about her experience in TV and her pivot into game design.

At this point in the evening, Jim steps away to fire up the Blackstones. Seared steak, sauteed vegetables, and chicken are on the menu. Andy, the co-founder of INTDEV, steps in to interview our final panelist, Ben (Roth), who is building a gaming platform called Castle. Castle is a mobile platform where people can make and post games from their phone. Or as Ben puts it, “It’s a place where people come to loiter on the internet.”

Andy and Ben speak of days of yore and what it was like growing up in the era of Newgrounds, Flash Games, and eBaum’s World, and how these early experiences on the internet inspired Ben to build Castle. Ben believes that “great games allow players to express themselves through the gameplay,” and that the biggest barrier to getting more people to make games is their mindset — not the tools, not coding ability — but the belief that they are someone who can create games. And by the looks of it, Castle has been quite convincing in this regard.

Again, Ben speaks of people’s love of loitering on the internet, and the room laughs at the deep truth contained in this statement. He explains how Castle has become a haven for people (especially young people) who love to loiter, and that this begets a recursive cycle of game design. In fact, some of the games made by Castle users have been “remixed” thousands and thousands of times. He mentions a game where a hamburger and its ingredients fall down the screen, plopping at the the bottom of the window. “Hamburger physics,” he calls it. He talks about another game where users can design and deploy mini-golf courses, full of obstacles and fun tchotchkes. Apparently, someone remixed these games, so that now, if one desires, they could play a putt-putt course where the only way to get the ball in the hole is to have it be carried by the aforementioned hamburger physics. Ahhh, yes, the beauty of loitering.

Visit castle.xyz to explore the game engine.

All this talk of hamburger physics, mixed with the smell of roasting meat wafting in from the front porch has everyone’s stomach grumbling. So we break for dinner and some friendly mixing and mingling. Because I don’t know anyone there, I spend the first part of this intermission admiring the work of Anna’s Bead Bodega. Fashioned out of wood and paint, Anna (Dong) has created “storefronts” that look like something out of Barbie Land. Imagine vertical white and pink stripes and a bubblegum awning to match. On shelves rest an assortment of BOLD beadwork. Bold does not entirely capture what this art does. Great art is hard to ignore. Anna has taken colorful jewel-like plastic beads and fashioned intricate 3D structures out of them. Prominently displayed are all-bead clutches, handbags, bucket hats, adornment boxes for your Kleenex, drink totes, earrings, candle holders and more. And of course, jumbo silk scrunchies that make me wish for long hair again. The work is intricate and truly speaks for itself. Love it or hate it, it’s unapologetically Anna. Big balls, indeed.

In a corner, on the other side of the Bead Bodega, I find Sai in conversation with a software engineer who keeps my MetaMask wallet secure. I join the convo to listen while I savor the delicious dinner generously prepared for us by the janitor. Maddie, in search of Sai, joins too, and I’m able to pick her brain for a while. I ask how she pivoted from TV to games and what the transition has been like. I’m curious to learn what differences she observes in compelling storytelling between the two industries.

Her take is that key elements of story — like characters, conflict, etc. — are maintained between the two art forms. And she points out that creative expression in each domain is greatly influenced, and ultimately determined, by the quality of relationships you have with teammates. It’s clear from her tone that the work she does with Sai and the team at Mesh brings her deep joy and challenge and growth. She tells me she quit TV because the people she worked with didn’t respect or support her as a creative — something I’m grappling with now in my current role. I confide in her that I’m facing an imminent career pivot and without hesitation she encourages me to make it happen. In fact, she tells me exactly what to do. Putting one finger up on each hand, she waves her arms, quickly crisscrossing them and booms, “Peace out! Sometimes it feels really, really good to quit!” I suspect that it’s this agency of Maddie’s that gives her an edge. We speak for only a few more minutes before the panel regroups.

This time all of the speakers gather in the front of the room. Some on the plush green sofa, others on the wooden chairs, adjacent. I sense a palpable shift in vibe after dinner. I guess delicious meat and vegetables makes us all a little bit more relaxed, a little bit more human.

The conversation resumes on the topic of game testing and how to get useful validation and feedback from people in the gaming community. Jeff’s take is that the “sweatiest sweat lords” make the best game testers, and Ben adds that he leverages players in Castle’s discord server to get feedback on revisions and updates. All agree that sometimes changes are not well-received and that sincere messages telling you to go kill yourself are not uncommon. This gets a big laugh, for we know all too well that people online can be brutal and cowardly under the veil of anonymity.

Jim jumps in with a disclaimer, “By the way, guys, it’s totally NOT okay to tell people to kill themselves.” We laugh some more.

Jeff adds a silver lining, “Hey, if you think about it, this person cared about my game soooo much that they took the time to send me a death threat when I changed their character’s height by a few inches.”

From here the discussion flips to the other side of the coin — positive feedback and encouragement that strangers can provide. Sai mentions how he has a file with a screenshot from a player that loves his game, and that he looks at this about every six months, and will continue to do so until the day he dies. Jeff agrees that small, positive nudges go a long way. When someone says, “Thanks for making this. I’m soooo happy I found this game,” that’s priceless.

When asked for his take on how positive and negative feedback plays out on Bluesky, Paul says, “Our moderation is pretty on the ball, so we don’t deal with toxic comments as often. But one time I changed how replies were sorted and people lost their minds!”

A fantastic question will come up later about how to make the “gamer” community less toxic. But before we get there, the speakers are asked about their favorite games. Paul claims his favorite is a hole-digging simulator. Yes, you heard right. In fact, it’s called “A Game About Digging a Hole.” The premise is simple: you dig the biggest hold you can muster in your very own (virtual) backyard. Maddie throws another equally bizarre response into the mix. One of her favorite games is what I presume to be The Crush House. I didn’t catch the name but she describes it as a game where players assume the role of a reality TV producer and camera person who follow people around a house, capturing Love-Island-style interactions. According to chatGPT, “the goal is to keep the show on the air by satisfying a diverse audience, which includes wine moms, voyeurs, and cinephiles.” Folks, 2025 is a wild time to be alive.

Paul jumps in and shares an interesting point that sometimes the most unintuitive changes to gameplay make the experience way better. We all share a good laugh over some vampire-slaying game that hilariously removed a fundamental mechanic — the ability to aim where you shoot! Spray and pray taken to the extreme of chaos and fun.

I decide to push the convo in a speculative direction, and from my notes, grab the curiosity: “What will games look like in 50 years?” Jeff claims, to the dismay of others, that he won’t be around in 50 years. Someone in the audience gasps and says, “No Jeff! Don’t go!” Paul echoes a lingering concern of climate apocalypse that lands us (at least those who manage to survive) in the Thunderdome. Sai is intrigued and takes the bait. He shares a belief that 2D screens will be a vestige of the past and that we will have advanced neural interfaces (i.e., brain-computer interfaces) that allow us to play games entirely in our minds. No more screens.

Paul agrees and posits we may have advanced mixed-reality tech by then or use haptics and sign language for rapid, in-game communication and command execution. “We’d effectively become wizards,” he says!

The next question changes the tone entirely — a curiosity I alluded to earlier. A woman sitting behind me asks, “How do we make the gaming community less toxic? It has to be one of the most toxic communities I’ve been part of.” I can tell she doesn’t make this claim lightly. The panelists’ faces turn somber as they think back to unpleasant experiences with strangers on the internet.

Ben starts the first thread. “If we want to make gamers less toxic, it’s not just about creating the right tools, but getting people to use them.” There is talk between audience and panel about AI agents and voice moderation. Jeff chimes in to express his belief that people will inherently push against AI moderation, which may feel intrusive. After all, there is a line between vitriol and talking mad shit to your friends — how is the AI to know? Ben concurs, once again pointing to the fact that strangers come to the internet to loiter with other loiterers — human loiterers, specifically. Any whiff of loitering with non-human agents takes the joy out of loitering entirely, he believes.

I most appreciate Maddie’s thoughts on the matter, as she offers a deeply human solution to this problem. She explains, the only way to eradicate toxicity is “by intentionally and persistently inviting more people from diverse backgrounds into gaming communities; and showing them that gaming is for them too.” A poignant belief, stated with conviction by the only woman on the panel.

The next question asker wants to know if they’ve ever heard of AAA game dev studios masquerading as indie studios to raise money or appeal to certain player audiences. Jeff retorts that “players don’t really care what house a game comes from, they care that the game is actually good. As for AAA studios, they have their cash cows,” he says. “They know what sells, so they’ll keep churning out more of the same.” Grand Theft Auto 7000, as Maddie puts it. Maddie then pitches a game about a reality tv show game where you POV as a AAAs producer trying to catfish investors and players by pretending to be indie. Surprise, motherfuckers. The panel agrees that “indie in gaming” may have an entirely different connotation than, say, “indie in music.”

By now it’s getting late, and to Blake’s dismay Jim keeps taking questions. After some quick back and forth with a person seated in the audience, Ben brings the devolving discussion to a close by saying, “So you’re asking: what is culture?” “We’ll have to save that for the next event,” says Blake.

And that’s where I leave it. I find my bag and water bottle and say my goodbyes. Jim tells me to read “Finite and Infinite Games” by Carse, and with this in mind I saunter off into the Seattle night. And over and over I flip something in my head. A thing that Jeff said.

He said that his dream is “for gaming to bring more joy into people’s lives by shifting the way they perceive the everyday, the mundane. It can change the way they move through the world. Down to how they think about opening a door, for example.”

And suddenly I find myself on an infinite sidewalk, stepping to the rhythm of Maggie Rogers banging in my headphones. I am become Gilgamesh, tamer of immortality.

tl;dr Paul’s probably digging a hole right now.
